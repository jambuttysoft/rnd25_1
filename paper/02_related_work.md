# 2. Related Work

## 2.1 Document Understanding with LLMs

Early work on automated document understanding used OCR pipelines combined with rule-based extraction, where template matching and regular expressions extracted fields from documents with known layouts. The move to neural approaches began with models that jointly encoded textual and visual features. LayoutLM (Xu et al., 2020) was an influential milestone: it incorporated both word embeddings and 2D spatial position embeddings derived from OCR bounding boxes, achieving strong performance on tasks such as form understanding (FUNSD) and receipt parsing (SROIE). Subsequent iterations—LayoutLMv2 (Xu et al., 2021) and LayoutLMv3 (Huang et al., 2022)—added image patch embeddings and a unified text-image pre-training objective, further improving accuracy on visually rich documents.

A parallel line of work sought to remove the dependency on a separate OCR engine entirely. Donut (Kim et al., 2022) introduced an end-to-end architecture that reads documents directly from image pixels using a vision encoder paired with an autoregressive text decoder. Trained on synthetic document data, Donut demonstrated competitive performance on document classification and information extraction without requiring any explicit OCR stage. This approach is appealing for receipts and invoices, which contain both printed and handwritten text, varied fonts, and occasionally poor scan quality.

More recently, general-purpose multimodal LLMs have subsumed much of this specialized work. Models such as GPT-4V (OpenAI, 2023), Gemini (Google DeepMind, 2023), and Qwen-VL (Bai et al., 2023) demonstrate broad capability on document understanding tasks without task-specific fine-tuning. For receipt processing specifically, these models can extract vendor names, dates, amounts, and line items from photographs of varying quality, in multiple languages, and from a wide range of receipt formats. However, these systems focus on field extraction and structured output generation; they do not evaluate the semantic justification of the extracted content relative to a policy context. Our work extends the document understanding pipeline by adding a downstream policy compliance layer that reasons over the extracted fields.

## 2.2 Policy Compliance in NLP

Automated compliance checking has been studied across several domains. In legal NLP, work on contract analysis frames compliance as a clause-level classification task: given a contract clause, does it satisfy a stated legal requirement? LexGLUE (Chalkidis et al., 2022) provides a benchmark across six legal NLP tasks, demonstrating that pre-trained language models can achieve reasonable performance on legal classification even without extensive legal domain pre-training. Contract-BERT and similar domain-adapted models have shown further gains. However, legal compliance checking typically operates on structured, formal text rather than the noisy, informal text found in expense receipts and project policy documents.

In regulatory compliance, Holzenberger et al. (2020) studied automated rule-following for US tax law, framing statutes as executable programs and testing whether NLP models could determine whether specific scenarios satisfied regulatory conditions. Their work highlighted a persistent challenge: natural language rules contain implicit conditions, exceptions, and interdependencies that are not surfaced by surface-level text matching. Similar challenges arise in our policy compliance setting, where expense categories must be matched to policy clauses that use varying vocabulary and implicit assumptions about scope.

Privacy policy compliance is another relevant strand of work. Wilson et al. (2016) and subsequent OPP-115 research demonstrated that machine learning models can classify policy statements into categories (data collection, sharing, retention) with reasonable accuracy, enabling automated auditing of privacy policies at scale. This line of work is notable for its focus on vague, legally hedged language—a property shared by the vague policy variant in our study.

Rule-following as an alignment property of LLMs has been studied by Ouyang et al. (2022) in the context of instruction following via reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Their work showed that instruction-tuned models substantially outperform base language models on tasks that require adherence to stated constraints. More recent work has examined LLM behavior under conflicting instructions and found that models frequently violate constraints when the conflict is implicit or when the constraint is underspecified (Sun et al., 2023). Our E6 adversarial experiment directly tests this property: under a contradictory policy, does the model flag the contradiction or proceed to a verdict as if the policy were coherent?

## 2.3 Chain-of-Thought Prompting

Wei et al. (2022) demonstrated that prompting LLMs to produce step-by-step reasoning chains before reaching a final answer—a technique they termed chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting—substantially improves performance on multi-step arithmetic, commonsense reasoning, and symbolic reasoning tasks, particularly for sufficiently large models. The key insight is that generating intermediate reasoning steps allows the model to decompose complex problems into sub-problems, each of which is more tractable. This effect emerged without any training signal: simply appending "Let's think step by step" or providing a few worked examples with explicit reasoning chains was sufficient to elicit the behavior.

Subsequent work has explored CoT in zero-shot settings (Kojima et al., 2022), showing that the "Let's think step by step" instruction alone, without few-shot examples, yields substantial gains. Wang et al. (2022) introduced self-consistency sampling—generating multiple CoT reasoning paths and taking a majority vote—which further improves accuracy at the cost of additional inference. These techniques have been applied across diverse tasks, but their application to compliance and justification tasks remains less explored.

A critical open question for our work is whether CoT improves not just accuracy but also explainability—the degree to which the stated reasoning correctly justifies the final verdict. A model might produce a correct verdict with incorrect reasoning (a form of post-hoc rationalization) or incorrect reasoning that reaches a correct conclusion by accident. In audit contexts, explainability is as important as accuracy: a verdict without a valid, grounded justification is not useful to a human reviewer. Our E2 versus E3 comparison tests whether CoT produces verdicts that are both more accurate and more faithfully grounded in the policy text.

Turpin et al. (2023) showed that chain-of-thought reasoning in LLMs can be systematically unfaithful—the model's stated reasoning does not always reflect its actual decision process, and CoT can be manipulated by biasing cues in the prompt. This finding is directly relevant to our audit setting: if the model's reasoning chain is not genuinely tracking policy compliance but rather reflecting training biases about what "legitimate" expenses look like, the pipeline provides false assurance. Our failure mode taxonomy in Section 5 documents observed cases of this kind.

## 2.4 Hallucination and Uncertainty in LLMs

A major practical concern for deploying LLMs in compliance roles is hallucination: the generation of confident, plausible-sounding but factually incorrect outputs (Ji et al., 2023). In policy compliance, hallucination can take the form of inventing policy clauses that were not present, misquoting amounts or dates from a receipt, or—most relevant to our E6 experiment—reporting confidence in a verdict when the underlying policy provides no clear basis for one. Kadavath et al. (2022) showed that LLMs have some capacity for calibrated uncertainty expression when directly asked, but that this calibration degrades under distribution shift and in low-resource settings.

Work on uncertainty quantification for NLP (Gal & Ghahramani, 2016; Desai & Durrett, 2020) has proposed both Bayesian and non-Bayesian approaches to estimating model uncertainty, but these methods are not directly applicable to black-box API-accessed LLMs. Our approach instead evaluates uncertainty expression through the structured output channel: we measure the rate at which models assign the `unclear` verdict label on receipts that human annotators flagged as ambiguous, treating this as a behavioral proxy for uncertainty calibration.

# 8. Conclusion

We presented an AI-driven pipeline for Policy Semantic Audit — the evaluation of expense justification against a natural-language project policy using zero-shot chain-of-thought reasoning. The pipeline operates as an interactive web application, enabling researchers and practitioners to upload real expense receipts and observe real-time LLM reasoning about policy compliance. By separating the pipeline into three layers — multimodal OCR extraction, policy grounding, and CoT-based reasoning — the architecture allows each component to be evaluated and replaced independently, and supports model-comparison experiments without code changes.

The central motivation for this work is the auditability gap in current expense management systems: structural validation of receipts is well-solved, but semantic justification — whether an expense is appropriate given the project's current goals and phase — requires natural language reasoning that rule-based systems cannot provide. Our experiments demonstrate that this gap can be narrowed using off-the-shelf LLMs and well-engineered prompts, with results that are auditable through the generated reasoning chains.

Our six-experiment evaluation on 26 real receipts addressed distinct capabilities: multimodal field extraction (E1), zero-shot verdict classification (E2), chain-of-thought reasoning (E3), phase-sensitive policy grounding (E4), uncertainty expression on ambiguous expenses (E5), and robustness under adversarial policies (E6). Beyond the quantitative results, the primary contribution of this study is the documented failure mode taxonomy — a systematic account of problems encountered at each pipeline stage and the solutions applied. This taxonomy includes JSON parsing failures in the extraction layer, policy clause hallucination in the reasoning layer, overconfident verdicts under contradictory policies, and the tendency of some models to produce reasoning chains that are structurally correct but not genuinely grounded in the policy text. These failure modes are not hypothetical; they were observed during implementation and are described in Section 5 with concrete examples. For researchers and practitioners building similar applied LLM pipelines, this documentation provides a practical guide to the most common failure patterns and their mitigations.

Several findings have immediate practical implications. First, the self-referential nature of this study — receipts from building an AI auditing system evaluated against the policy governing that project — provides ecological validity that is unusual in applied NLP research. The policy variants are grounded in real project activities, and the verdicts have genuine interpretive weight. Second, the three-model comparison (Gemini 2.5 Flash, Qwen2.5-72B, Mixtral-8x7B) at different scales and cost points provides a practical model-selection framework for organizations considering deployment. Third, the adversarial policy experiments (E6) highlight a risk that is easy to overlook: a pipeline that works well under a clear, well-structured policy may degrade severely under the vague or inconsistent policies that are common in real organizations.

The limitations of this study — dataset size, single annotator, synthetic policy, single domain — are substantial and constrain the generalizability of the quantitative findings. They do not, however, diminish the methodological contribution: a replicable experimental design, a failure mode taxonomy, and an open research artifact that enables direct replication and extension. Future work should prioritize a larger multi-annotator dataset, integration with live policy documents, and a systematic evaluation across languages and organizational domains.

The broader question this study opens is whether LLM-based semantic compliance can be made reliable enough for production audit use. The answer from this study is: not yet, unconditionally — but the failure modes are identifiable, the accuracy under clear policies is promising, and the pipeline generates auditable reasoning chains that support rather than replace human judgment. Policy Semantic Audit is not a replacement for human financial reviewers; it is a first-pass filter and explanation generator that allows reviewers to direct their attention to the cases that most need it.
